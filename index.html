Myth or fact?

Each team holds up its Myth or Fact card on “3, 2, 1”. One point per correct team, then open the answer. Ask one team to explain before you reveal.

01“Accessible products are ugly and boring.”

Myth. Wise redesigned its design system around accessibility, with a new accessible palette, more legible type and lower cognitive load, and the result looks better. Constraints like 4.5:1 contrast rule out a few greys, not good taste.

02“If contrast and sizes are right, the product is accessible.”

Myth. Visual rules are a fraction of WCAG. Keyboard use, focus, reading order, structure, labels, timing, alternatives to gestures and semantics all matter, and most of them are invisible in a static mockup.

03“Disabled users are a small niche.”

Myth. 1 in 6 people, and the population is ageing fast. Add situational limits and everyone hits these barriers at some point.

04“Designing for accessibility makes things worse for everyone else.”

Myth. The curb-cut effect: curb ramps were fought for by wheelchair users and now serve strollers, suitcases and bikes. Captions, clear labels, bigger targets and good structure make products easier for everyone, and help SEO too.

05“Accessibility is the developers’ job.”

Myth. Developers can’t fix a colour palette, a drag-only interaction or an unlabelled icon they were handed. Designers decide most of what ends up failing. That’s what “shift left” means.

06“An accessibility widget or overlay will make us compliant.”

Myth. Overlays add a toolbar on top of the site, but they can’t fix missing labels, broken focus order or inaccessible custom components, and they often clash with the screen readers people already use. Many disabled users and accessibility experts actively advise against them. The fix lives in the design and the code.

The framework

You don’t need to memorise 86 criteria. You need four questions.

WCAG 2.2 has 86 success criteria, and 55 of them make up the AA level most laws require. They’re organised around four principles: perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. Turned into questions, they become a review you can run on any screen in five minutes. The rest of the session takes them one at a time.

Words you’ll hear · 1 min

A quick dictionary, so nobody gets lost

1.4.3 · AA

Every rule in this session carries a tag like this. The number is the WCAG success criterion, and the letters are its level: A is the minimum, AA is what laws like the EAA ask for, and AAA is the strictest. Best practice means it’s not a WCAG rule, but experts recommend it.

WCAG
Web Content Accessibility Guidelines: the international standard from the W3C. Version 2.2 is the latest.
Screen reader
Software that reads the interface aloud or sends it to a braille display. VoiceOver, TalkBack, NVDA, JAWS.
Focus
Which element the keyboard is on right now. The focus state is how you show it.
Accessible name
What a control is called for assistive tech. Usually its visible text, sometimes written in the annotations.
Landmarks
The big regions of a page (header, navigation, main, footer) that screen reader users can jump between.
Alt text
The text alternative for an image, read out instead of the picture.
Announcement
Something the screen reader says when content changes. Polite waits for a pause, assertive interrupts.
ARIA
Extra code that tells assistive tech what custom components are and do. Developers add it, designers specify the intent.
Annotations
Notes on your frames that tell developers what the code has to say: headings, names, order, announcements.

    A · Four ideas and a demo · 3 min

    How assistive tech sees your design

    01

    It reads code, not pixels.

    The browser turns HTML into an accessibility tree. That tree, not your frame, is what screen readers, voice control and switch users interact with.

    02

    Name, role, value.

    Every control is announced by what it’s called, what it is and what state it’s in: “Continue, button, dimmed”. If the design doesn’t define the name, nobody will.

    03

    • Annotate traits and names in platform words: “Header”, “Button, selected”, “Adjustable”.

    • Fixed-height cells and tab bars that truncate when text grows.
    • Icon-only tab bars with no labels.
    • Bottom sheets and modals that VoiceOver or TalkBack can read behind.
    • Copying web specs to an app without checking platform patterns first.

    Ask who designs for apps. Ask them to open Settings › Accessibility on their own phone, set text to the largest size and open the last app they designed.     Beyond the product · reference

    Decks, PDFs and emails count too

    As an agency we ship a lot more than screens: proposals, reports, presentations, newsletters. The same laws cover many of them, and the same people read them.

    PDFs

    Export isn’t enough.

    PDFs exported from Figma aren’t tagged, so screen readers get little or no structure. For documents people must read, build the final version in a tool that exports tagged PDFs, or publish it as a web page. Check it with Acrobat’s accessibility checker or the free PAC tool.

    Decks

    Real text, real titles.

    A unique title on every slide, live text instead of text in images, alt text on charts, reading order set, and the same contrast rules. Shared decks should work without the speaker.

    Emails

    Never one big image.

    Live text at 16px or more, alt text on every image, descriptive link text, real buttons, and a layout that still makes sense when images are blocked.

    Everything

    Same four questions.

    Can everyone perceive it, operate it, understand it, and can the file say it? Language set, headings marked, links that make sense alone.

    Team debate · 5 min +2 for the best argument

    Hard calls: what senior designers actually argue about

    Rules are the easy part. The real work is the trade-offs with brand, product and deadlines. Each team picks a card and has 90 seconds to argue what they’d do. Then open the senior answer and compare.

    01 · Brand“Our brand colour fails contrast with white text.”

    Don’t change the brand, change how it’s used. Keep the colour for large surfaces, fills and accents, and put dark text on it (black on Nacar pink is 6.3:1). Add a darker “text” variant token for links and small text, document both in the design system, and show brand the numbers side by side. Brand teams say yes to evidence, not to rules.

    02 · Deadline“Ship now, we’ll fix accessibility later.”

    Agree on non-negotiables, ticket the rest. Keyboard access, labels, contrast and focus go into the Definition of Done, because later costs up to 30× more and rarely happens. Everything else becomes a ticket with a severity and an owner. Mention that since June 2025 it’s a legal requirement for e-commerce in the EU, then move on. Fear doesn’t build habits, process does.

    03 · Stakeholder“The CEO loves the auto-playing hero carousel.”

    Keep it, but make it behave. No autoplay, or autoplay with a visible pause. Previous and next buttons of at least 24px, slide labels (“2 of 4”), keyboard support, and every slide reachable without swiping. Then show the analytics for slides 2 to 4 and let the data have the next conversation.

    04 · Pattern“Disable the button until the form is valid.”

    Usually, don’t. A disabled button is exempt from contrast, but it doesn’t say what’s missing, and screen reader users may never find out why nothing happens. Keep it enabled, validate on submit, and show an error summary that links to each field. If you must disable it, say what’s missing right next to it.

    05 · Density“It’s a dense enterprise tool. No room for labels.”

    Small labels beat no labels. Put 12px labels above or to the left of fields rather than relying on placeholders. Icon-only toolbars are fine with an accessible name and a tooltip on focus for each button, plus enough spacing to meet the target size exception. Power users get speed, and everyone still knows what each control is.

    06 · Theming“For dark mode we just inverted the palette.”

    Every theme is a new palette to check. Give each theme its own tokens, and check text, borders, focus and every state in both. Inverted greys rarely land on the same ratios, and pure white on pure black can feel harsh for long reading. Off-white on near-black is easier on the eyes.

    Facilitator notes

    This is where seniors earn their keep. Push back on each team’s answer once (“what if the PM still says no?”) before revealing. Award points for the most convincing argument, not for matching the card. There’s rarely one right answer, just better and worse trade-offs.

    Objection“Our users don’t have disabilities.”

    Objection“Let’s do it in phase 2.”

    Objection“Can’t we just install an accessibility plugin?”

    Team quiz · 4 min +1 each
    01What contrast does 16px regular body text need?

    4.5:1 (1.4.3, AA). 3:1 is only for large text, 24px regular or about 18.7px bold. 7:1 is AAA.

    02A disabled button’s label has 2:1 contrast. Does it fail WCAG?

    Inactive components are exempt. Do make it clear why the button is disabled, or avoid disabling it at all.

    03Which of these is not enough to show a form error?

    Colour alone fails 1.4.1, and errors must be described in text (3.3.1).

    04What is the minimum target size in WCAG 2.2 AA?

    24×24 CSS px (2.5.8). 44×44 is the best practice, especially on touch, and is the AAA criterion.

    05A playlist can only be reordered by dragging. What’s missing?

    2.5.7 Dragging movements (AA): every drag needs a single-tap or click alternative.

    06A modal closes. Where should keyboard focus go?

    Back to the trigger, so users carry on where they were. Annotate it, because it doesn’t happen by default with custom modals.

    07What is the best alt text for a header logo that links to the home page?

    It’s a functional image, so name the destination. Never say “image”, because the role already says it.

    08How do you choose a heading level?

    Hierarchy, not appearance. One H1, no skipped levels. Visual style and semantic level are separate decisions.

    09A “Saved” snackbar appears but doesn’t take focus. What should the annotation say?

    4.1.3 Status messages (AA). Success is polite. Keep assertive for critical errors.

    10What belongs in a designer’s accessibility annotations?

    Annotate the intent: names, roles (button or link), headings, order and announcements. Developers choose the implementation.

    11Automated tools like axe report zero issues on your page. What can you conclude?

    Automated tools catch only part of the issues. Focus order, meaningful alt text, link purpose and most of the interaction criteria need a person, a keyboard and a screen reader.

    12A product page shows “Only 2 left!” and a 4:59 offer countdown. Who does this hurt most, and what would you do?

    Fake scarcity and countdowns add pressure and anxiety, especially for people with ADHD, autism or anxiety. Honest stock and delivery information works better for everyone, and the timer may also fail 2.2.1 if it’s real.

    • Focus order defined where the layout breaks it
    • Focus never hidden by sticky elements
    • Modals: focus in, Esc closes, focus returns
    • Tap alternatives for drag, swipe, pinch
    • Pause for motion; time limits extendable
    • No fake urgency or surprise pop-ups

    Understand and hand off

    • Headings by hierarchy; landmarks; page title
    • Button or link, for each control
    • Names for icons and vague links
    • Alt text, or marked decorative
    • Visible labels, instructions, error text
    • Announcements: what, polite or assertive
    • Page language set; layouts survive translation
